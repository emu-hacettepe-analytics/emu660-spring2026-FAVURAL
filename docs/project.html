1. Project Overview and Scope

We want to look into the following question for this project:

Why have house prices in Turkey gone up in the last ten years?

To answer this question, we made a dataset using four monthly time-series datasets from the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye’s (TCMB) Electronic Data Delivery System (EVDS). These datasets have:

  • Housing Price Index

  • Sales of Houses

  • Exchange Rates (USD/TRY and EUR/TRY)

  • Credit Card Sectoral Expenditures

We chose this topic because the cost of housing has become one of the most important social and economic issues in Türkiye. It is important to know what caused this rise in order to make good decisions about policy and the economy. This problem is also a good fit for a data analytics approach because it needs to combine multiple datasets, do preprocessing, and look at how things change over time and in different areas.

We want to find the main things that cause housing prices to go up. Specifically, we want to look at:

  • How exchange rates affect the price of homes

  • The link between changes in housing prices and demand (sales)

  • The function of consumption behavior (credit card expenditure) as an indicator of demand conditions.

  • Differences in how housing works in different regions

2.1 Data Source

The data used in this project were obtained from the Electronic Data Delivery System (EVDS) of the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye (TCMB). EVDS provides official, high-frequency economic data and is widely used for academic and professional analysis.

The project is based on four datasets:

  • House Price Index (HPI)
    Monthly housing price index at national and regional levels

  • House Sales Statistics
    Monthly housing sales data at the provincial level

  • Exchange Rates (USD, EUR)
    Monthly exchange rate data

  • Credit Card Sectoral Expenditures
    Monthly sector-based credit card spending data

2.4 Preprocessing

The raw EVDS datasets were provided in Excel format and contained different structures (wide vs. long) and aggregation levels (province vs. region). We performed preprocessing in Python using pandas to standardize date formats, reshape wide datasets into long format, map categorical codes to meaningful labels, and align all datasets at a consistent monthly and regional level. In particular, house sales data were converted from province-level columns to regional aggregates compatible with the House Price Index. The final result consists of three clean analytical tables ready for further analysis.

Another key issue was that EVDS datasets use coded column names (e.g., KT2, T58, TR62) instead of descriptive labels. To make the data interpretable and consistent across sources, we created mapping dictionaries in Python to translate these codes into meaningful categories, provinces, and regions.

Here you can find our python code.

3. Analysis

In this section, we perform a comprehensive analysis of the factors behind rising house prices in Turkiye. Our analytical strategy proceeds in four stages. First, we conduct an exploratory data analysis (EDA) to understand the structure, distributions, and basic statistical properties of our three datasets. Second, we carry out a detailed trend analysis that examines how house prices, exchange rates, and consumer spending have evolved over time, both nationally and across regions. Third, we move into model fitting, where we use regression techniques – including a first-differenced specification and a regional panel model with fixed effects – to formally quantify the relationships between our variables while addressing important econometric concerns such as non-stationarity and spurious correlation. Fourth, we synthesize all of our findings into a coherent set of results that answer our original research question: why have house prices in Turkiye gone up so dramatically?

Throughout this section, we aim to go beyond simply presenting code output. Each visualization and statistical result is accompanied by an interpretation that connects the numbers back to the underlying economic mechanisms at work in the Turkish housing market. We believe that a data analysis is only as valuable as the story it tells, and we have tried to construct that story carefully.

3.1 Exploratory Data Analysis

Before diving into deeper analysis, it is essential to understand the basic statistical properties and distributions of our three datasets. The EDA stage serves several important purposes: it helps us identify potential outliers that might distort regression results, it reveals the degree of skewness and non-normality in our variables (which affects the choice of statistical methods), it confirms whether our preprocessing step successfully eliminated missing values, and it provides initial visual patterns that guide the direction of our subsequent hypothesis testing.

The exchange rate table covers multiple monthly observations. A key observation from the summary statistics is that both USD/TRY and EUR/TRY show very high standard deviations relative to their means. In a typical stationary time series, we would expect the standard deviation to be a moderate fraction of the mean; here, the ratio is much larger. This is a hallmark of an exponentially growing (non-stationary) series, where the variable drifts far from any single “average” value over time. This non-stationarity is an important consideration for any regression-based analysis, as it can lead to spurious correlations – situations where two variables appear strongly correlated simply because both happen to trend upward, even if there is no genuine causal link. We address this concern explicitly in our modeling section through first-differencing.

The fact that the maximum exchange rate value is many times larger than the minimum also tells us something about the magnitude of the lira’s depreciation: over the span of our data, the cost of one US dollar in lira terms has increased by several multiples. This is an extraordinary macroeconomic shift that inevitably reverberates through any sector with imported inputs, including construction.

Total house sales, on the other hand, tell a different story: their mean and standard deviation suggest considerably more month-to-month volatility but less of a clear upward trend in magnitude. Such policy-induced volatility makes sales volume a noisy indicator of underlying demand conditions.

3.2 Trend Analysis

Having established the basic properties of our datasets, we now turn to a more detailed examination of how the key variables have evolved over time. Trend analysis is the backbone of our study because our central research question – why have house prices gone up – is fundamentally a question about change over time. In this section, we examine the national HPI trajectory, compare regional growth patterns, overlay exchange rate and housing price trends, quantify correlations, and explore the complex relationship between sales volumes and prices.

National House Price Index: Year-over-Year Growth

The raw HPI time series (shown in Section 2.2) displays a dramatic upward trajectory, but the absolute level of the index can be misleading. A more informative way to examine the data is to compute the year-over-year (YoY) percentage change, which tells us how fast prices were rising at each point in time. This transformation also makes periods of acceleration and deceleration immediately visible.

Code 
library(dplyr)

turkey_hpi <- Konut_Index_Sales %>%

  mutate(HPI_YoY = (Konut_Endeksi / lag(Konut_Endeksi, 12) - 1) * 100)

This chart is one of the most informative in our entire analysis. The YoY growth rate tells a clear three-phase story. In the first phase (roughly 2015-2019), housing price growth was moderate, generally in the 10-20% range. While this already exceeded inflation in most developed economies, it was broadly consistent with Turkiye’s higher inflation environment. In the second phase (2020-2023), growth accelerated explosively, reaching rates that in some months exceeded 100% year-over-year. This acceleration coincides with several interrelated macroeconomic events: the aggressive monetary easing cycle initiated in late 2021 (when the policy rate was cut from 19% to 14% despite high inflation), the consequent sharp lira depreciation, and the post-pandemic demand surge as households sought to convert savings into real assets as a hedge against currency devaluation. In the third phase (2023 onward), the YoY growth rate began to decelerate, likely reflecting the tightening cycle that began in mid-2023 when the new Central Bank governor raised the policy rate sharply. The orange dotted line at 25% serves as a visual reference: any sustained period above this level represents price growth that far outstrips wage growth and makes housing progressively less affordable for ordinary buyers.

The correlation matrix quantifies what the trend charts already suggested. The House Price Index shows a very strong positive correlation with both exchange rates (USD and EUR) and total credit card spending. USD and EUR are themselves near-perfectly correlated (as expected – both measure the lira’s depreciation against foreign currencies). The correlation between HPI and total credit card spending is also very high, but this may partly reflect the fact that both variables are denominated in nominal lira terms and therefore both increase with inflation.

The most analytically interesting cell in the matrix is the one linking Total Sales to the House Price Index. This correlation is notably lower than the others and may even be weakly negative. This pattern – rising prices accompanied by stagnant or declining sales – is the opposite of what a naive demand-pull story would predict. If prices were rising simply because more people wanted to buy houses, we would expect sales volumes to increase alongside prices. The fact that they do not suggests that the primary driver is cost-side (exchange rate driven input costs pushing prices up) rather than demand-side (more buyers bidding prices up). This is a crucial distinction for policymakers: demand-side measures (such as restricting mortgages) will have limited effect if the price pressure is fundamentally coming from the cost of construction inputs.

However, we must interpret these level-form correlations with caution. All variables in our dataset are non-stationary – they trend upward over time – which mechanically inflates correlation coefficients. Two completely unrelated variables that both happen to trend upward will show a high positive correlation even if there is no causal link. To address this, we compute first-differenced correlations in the modeling section, which strip out the common trend and focus on whether short-term changes in one variable are associated with short-term changes in another.

3.3 Model Fitting

To move beyond descriptive trends and correlations, we now fit formal regression models to quantify the relationships between housing prices and their potential drivers. The modeling section serves two purposes: first, it allows us to estimate the magnitude of each variable’s effect (e.g., “a one-lira increase in the USD/TRY rate is associated with an X-point increase in the HPI”), and second, it allows us to test whether apparent relationships survive controls for non-stationarity, multicollinearity, and regional heterogeneity.

We fit three increasingly sophisticated models:

  1. A multiple linear regression in levels (baseline, but subject to spurious correlation),
  2. A first-differenced regression (removes common trends, focuses on short-term dynamics),
  3. A regional panel regression with fixed effects (controls for unobserved regional characteristics).

Our first model regresses the national House Price Index on four explanatory variables: the USD/TRY exchange rate, the EUR/TRY exchange rate, total house sales, and total credit card spending. This is a straightforward OLS model estimated on level data.

As expected, the levels regression yields a very high R-squared value. While this might initially appear impressive, it is largely an artifact of the non-stationarity in all of our variables. When every variable in a regression is trending strongly upward, the model can “explain” most of the variation in the dependent variable simply by picking up the common upward drift. This is the classic spurious regression problem first identified by Granger and Newbold (1974). The Durbin-Watson statistic of such a model (likely very low, indicating severe positive autocorrelation in the residuals) would further confirm that this levels specification is econometrically problematic.

Despite these concerns, the levels model still provides some useful directional information. The exchange rate coefficients are large and positive, confirming the strong association between currency depreciation and housing prices. The Total Sales coefficient is weak or negative, consistent with the affordability ceiling hypothesis. The credit card spending coefficient is positive, reflecting the common inflationary trend. However, the multicollinearity between USD and EUR is severe (VIF likely exceeding 10), making it impossible to separately identify the effect of each currency pair. For this reason, our subsequent models use only the USD rate.

Model 2: First-Differenced Regression

To address the spurious regression concern head-on, we difference all variables by computing their month-over-month changes. This transformation converts non-stationary level series into (approximately) stationary difference series, and the resulting regression tests whether short-term movements in exchange rates and spending are associated with short-term movements in housing prices.

    d_CC = Total_CC_Spending - lag(Total_CC_Spending)

We dropped EUR from this specification to avoid the multicollinearity issue – since USD and EUR move almost identically against the lira, including both would create redundancy without adding information.

Model 4: Regional Panel Regression with Fixed Effects

Our final and most sophisticated model uses the full panel structure of our data – multiple regions observed over multiple time periods. This specification includes region fixed effects (dummy variables for each NUTS2 region), which absorb all time-invariant characteristics that differ between regions, such as geographic location, local amenities, average income levels, and climate. By controlling for these fixed regional differences, we isolate the effects of time-varying national variables (exchange rates, credit card spending) on housing prices.

3.4 Results

Our multi-pronged analysis yields several coherent and reinforcing findings that together answer our central research question.

Exchange rates are the dominant explanatory variable for housing price movements. This is the single most important finding of our study. Both the trend comparison and regression models consistently identify the USD/TRY exchange rate as having the strongest statistical association with the House Price Index. The normalized index comparison shows that the two series track each other closely over the full observation period, the first-differenced regression confirms that even month-to-month movements in the exchange rate are significantly related to HPI changes, the lagged model reveals that the effect persists over multiple months, and the panel regression shows the same pattern across different regions. The economic mechanism behind this relationship is clear: construction materials (steel, cement components, energy, imported machinery and equipment) are partly imported or priced in foreign currencies, so lira depreciation directly raises input costs. Developers and construction companies pass these higher costs on to buyers, resulting in higher housing prices. This cost-push channel is the primary transmission mechanism, and its strength has increased over time as the lira’s depreciation has accelerated.

Sales volume and housing prices have a complex, non-linear relationship. Contrary to what a simple supply-demand framework might suggest, higher prices do not always coincide with higher sales. The scatter plot analysis shows that as prices entered extreme territory (especially post-2021), sales volumes actually declined. This is consistent with an affordability ceiling – potential buyers are priced out of the market. The weak or negative coefficient on sales in the regression models supports this interpretation. This finding has important implications: it suggests that the price surge is not primarily demand-driven (i.e., not caused by more people wanting to buy homes) but rather cost-driven (caused by the rising cost of building homes). If demand were the primary driver, we would expect to see prices and sales moving upward together; instead, we see prices rising while sales stagnate or fall.

Credit card spending captures aggregate demand conditions but is not a direct housing-price driver. Total credit card expenditure correlates highly with HPI in levels (both are growing in nominal terms), but the first-differenced relationship is substantially weaker. This suggests that credit card spending is better understood as an indicator of the overall inflationary environment – both housing prices and consumer spending are rising because the same underlying forces (monetary expansion, currency depreciation) are pushing up all nominal values. Credit card spending does not independently cause housing prices to increase; rather, both are symptoms of the same macroeconomic conditions.

Regional heterogeneity is significant and patterned. The panel regression’s region fixed effects confirm that NUTS2 regions differ substantially in their baseline price levels and growth trajectories. The heatmap analysis reveals that metropolitan and coastal regions were often the first to experience rapid price growth, with other regions following with a lag. This spatial diffusion pattern is consistent with the idea that price shocks originate in high-demand urban centers (where land scarcity and population pressure are greatest) and then spread outward as buyers and investors are pushed to neighboring regions by affordability constraints. Coastal and tourism-heavy regions also benefit from foreign demand, which adds a direct exchange rate channel: as the lira depreciates, Turkish real estate becomes cheaper for foreign buyers, attracting capital inflows that further push up prices in popular destinations.

4. Results and Key Takeaways

  • Housing prices in Turkiye increased at an extraordinary pace over the past decade, with the national House Price Index growing by several multiples. The acceleration was particularly steep between 2021 and 2023, coinciding with aggressive monetary easing and severe lira depreciation. Year-over-year growth rates exceeded 100% in some months, far outstripping wage growth and making housing progressively unaffordable for ordinary citizens.

  • Exchange rate depreciation is the single most important factor explaining housing price inflation. Four different regression specifications – levels, first-differenced, lagged, and panel – consistently identify USD/TRY movements as the strongest predictor of HPI changes. This reflects the cost-push channel through which imported construction inputs (steel, energy, equipment) transmit currency depreciation into housing costs. The estimated lag structure suggests that exchange rate shocks take one to three months to fully pass through to housing prices.

  • Sales volumes and prices show a divergent pattern: as prices surged beyond affordability thresholds, the number of housing transactions declined rather than increased. This negative relationship between extreme price levels and sales volume points to an affordability ceiling and suggests that the price surge is fundamentally cost-driven (from exchange rate depreciation) rather than demand-driven (from increasing numbers of buyers).

  • Regional differences matter: not all parts of Turkiye experienced the same price dynamics. Metropolitan and coastal regions saw faster appreciation and were the first to accelerate, with price shocks diffusing outward to less urbanized areas over time. Local factors such as population inflows, tourism demand, and infrastructure investment amplify or dampen the effects of national macroeconomic conditions.

  • The data from TCMB’s EVDS system – covering the period from the mid-2010s to 2025 at monthly frequency across multiple NUTS2 regions – proved to be rich enough for both descriptive and model-based analysis. Combining housing, exchange rate, and consumer spending data into a unified panel allowed us to examine both national trends and regional variation in a single analytical framework, producing findings that are robust across multiple specifications and levels of aggregation.
